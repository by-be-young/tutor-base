# Choked by overtourism

Lincoln's Rock has long been one of the quiet spots in the Blue Mountains, a mountain range in Australia celebrated for its dramatic cliffs and beautiful forests. But in recent years, this once peaceful site has become a major tourist attraction.

Local officials witnessed a rising number of visitors flocking to the site, not for its scenery, but to recreate a photo taken by K-pop star Kim Jennie of Blackpink. In 2023, Kim's image of herself sitting on the edge of the cliff swept across social media. Since then, visitor numbers have increased sharply, bringing safety fears and environmental concerns. In late January, the Blue Mountains City Council temporarily closed access to the rock while officials worked on a response plan.

Lincoln's Rock isn't alone in its fight against overtourism, as social media fame has led to a growing list of global destinations facing problems like overcrowding. In Japan, for example, large numbers of tourists visited a small town near Mount Fuji to recreate a popular photo. This caused so many problems that the local government put up a black barrier to block the famous view.

"Being able to travel to and post about visiting the same places as one's social group or online connections can be a form of social status. # History in the ice

The "coolest" library on Earth has opened in Antarctica, but it doesn't have any books!

In January, the Ice Memory Sanctuary was established by the Ice Memory Foundation, which is led by scientists from France, Italy and other countries. The world's first facility for storing mountain ice cores, the sanctuary is essentially a cave dug into the snow.

> 1. 将上面这一段的最后一句中的"for storing"改为非谓语作定语，而非介宾结构作定语。
> 【@7】

Ice cores are like giant popsicles drilled from glaciers or ice sheets. Scientists have been collecting them for over 50 years. The sanctuary keeps the ice cores at around -50 C without using refrigerators. This saves energy and avoids problems like power failures or economic issues, according to the World Meteorological Organization (WMO).

Ice coring is important for understanding Earth's climate history. Like tree rings, ice cores have layers that show what the environment was like when each layer of snow fell, according to NASA. Snowflakes often crystallize around particles in the air, like dust, pollen, or sea salt. As snow builds up, these particles stay in the ice for thousands of years, offering clues about events like volcanic eruptions. The ice also traps air, creating "fossil" air pockets over time. Scientists can use this information to build computer models and predict future climate changes.

> 【@8】

Beyond environmental studies, ice cores also shine a light on human civilization. For example, in 2025, a Danish team studied 3,000-year-old ice cores from Greenland. By analyzing the air bubbles inside, they found lead pollution from ancient silver mining, reported NBC. In light of today's data on lead exposure, the team believed lead pollution might have lowered the average IQ in ancient Rome by 2.5 to 3 points, possibly contributing to the fall of the Roman Empire.

In short, ice cores "allow scientists - now and in the future - to understand what changed, how fast, and why. This is not nostalgia. This is preparation," Celeste Saulo, WMO secretary-general, said at the sanctuary's opening. However, Earth is losing 273 billion tons of ice annually, which is equal to 30 years of global human water use, according to the WMO. By the end of the century, nearly half of the world's glaciers could disappear, taking with them historical records.

# Caught in the AI loop

Public debate about AI in higher education has largely orbited a familiar worry: cheating. However, focusing so much on cheating misses a greater risk of AI: It is damaging the ecosystem of learning on which universities are built.

AI is shaping day-to-day academic work. There is a range of tools, including AI-assisted tutoring chatbots. Students use them as tutors. Faculty use them to draft lectures. Researchers use them to summarize papers and design experiments. They make universities look more productive, but they also bring many problems.

One problem is transparency. A student reading feedback on a term paper needs to know whether it was written by their instructor or by a robot. Anything less than complete transparency brings uncertainty, anxiety and distrust for students.

> 1. 缩句上面这一段的第二句（保留冠词等以维持语法严谨性）。你删掉的部分各是作什么成分？
> 【@14】

A second problem relates to intellectual credit. For example, if an instructor uses AI to draft an assignment and a student uses AI to write their answer, who is doing the evaluating, and what exactly is being evaluated?

The most consequential changes may come with AI systems that look less like assistants and more like agents. In practice, this means professors would still oversee classes, but the daily work of instruction would be managed by efficient, automated systems. In research, the path leads to completely automated processes, as seen in robotic labs that conduct experiments around the clock and even decide what test next.

This may sound like a good way to become more productive. But universities are not information factories; they are systems of practice. They depend on graduate students and early-career academics who develop their teaching and research skills by actually doing the work themselves. If AI takes on more of the "routine" responsibilities, it damages the very pathway through which new experts are developed.

The same dynamic applies to undergraduates. AI systems are offloading the most challenging parts of learning, which can help students build a deeper understanding. Students grow intellectually through doing the work of drafting, revising, failing, trying again, dealing with confusion and revising weak arguments. This is the work of learning how to learn.
