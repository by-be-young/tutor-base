# Fair play on thin ice

At the Milano-Cortina 2026 Winter Olympic Games, the United States won gold medals in both men's and women's ice hockey. However, this historic moment soon became overshadowed by controversy.

Following their win, the men's team received a call from US President Donald Trump, congratulating them. During the call, Trump invited them to his State of the Union address. He later joked that if he didn't invite the women, he risked getting impeached. This was met with laughs from the players but, according to ABC News, strong criticism from fans.

Two days later, Trump said, "I've never seen a goaltender play as well as Connor Hellebuyck" at the address, referring to the goalie of the US men's team. However, according to Harper's Bazaar magazine, Aerin Frankel, the goalie for the US women's team, let in as many goals in the entire Olympics as Hellebuyck did during the gold-medal game alone.

In Trump's words, the worst stereotypes about women's athletics were put on display. Despite being the best in the world and having the same achievements as their male counterparts, women in sport are still seen as less than, with their achievements treated as second-class.

# Boost your lifespan

Sleeping five more minutes, exercising 1.9 more minutes, and eating healthier each day could add a year to your life. This isn't just wishful thinking – it's the conclusion of a new study led by researchers from Australia and the UK.

"We always think that we need to make these massive overhauls," Nicholas Koemel, who led the study, told the magazine Scientific American. But "tweaks add up to make something meaningful."

For about eight years, the team followed 59,078 people in the UK. All participants provided information about their lifestyles and health, and some wore wristwatches that tracked their sleep and activity levels. Researchers evaluated everyone's diet using a scoring system ranging from 0 to 100 across 10 categories, with higher scores indicating healthier diets. For example, eating at least three servings of fruit per day earned the maximum 10 points in the fruit category. Based on these data, researchers estimated approximate changes in lifespan using scientific modeling, NBC News reported.

> 1. 将上面这一段第三句（Researchers ...）改为独立主格。（提示：只需删除一个词即可）类似地，分析倒数第二段第二句如何改为独立主格。
>    【@】

The results showed that people who sleep 5.5 hours per night, exercise 7.3 minutes per day and have a diet quality score of 36.9 could theoretically live a year longer with just five additional minutes of sleep, 1.9 minutes of vigorous or moderate exercise and half an extra serving of vegetables each day. If improving all three areas is too challenging, either 25 extra minutes of sleep per night or an additional 2.3 minutes of exercise per day can also help.

According to the study, patterns of sleep, physical activity and nutrition are well-known contributors to early death and non-communicable diseases. Traditionally, they've been studied separately, without considering how they influence each other or work together to affect health. However, "these findings highlight the importance of considering lifestyle behaviors as a package rather than in isolation," Koemel told CNN.

Professor Kevin McConway from the Open University in the UK argued that the study didn't clearly describe its statistical methods. This makes it difficult to determine whether the findings are due to the data itself or the researchers' choice of analyses. Koemel told NBC News that "the message here should not necessarily be that making these small tweaks is a silver bullet". Instead, it's about taking the first step towards creating sustainable and achievable opportunities for more people.

# Connected by post

Returning home after an exhausting day, I open my letterbox to find a colourful postcard with a short message written: "Happy Birthday, Minoli aunt." I put down my bags and breathe.

As I travel the world, postcards have become a way to tell people I love that I'm thinking of them. It's not a picture on Instagram or a social media message that gets lost among notifications. A postcard is tangible. It's real.

Sending a postcard is a labor of love and picking the perfect one is an art – it has to capture the essence of the place and how I feel while there. The image says something about the connection between me and my recipient. Deciding what to write and putting pen to paper is therapeutic. It makes the recipient a part of my day.

Then there's the act of getting the stamps, finding an open post office, stopping by a postbox and sliding in the postcard, hoping it gets delivered. There's no notification indicating delivery. It's out there in the world, beyond my control.

Postcards are short messages between two people, but a whole community helps deliver them. As a child, I loved finding postcards in my parents' photo albums. They told stories of people I knew or had never met, sparking my imagination. In an age when meaningful dialogue seems increasingly rare, postcards quietly preserve something beyond words. It wasn't until the COVID-19 pandemic, when I was living on my own away from friends, family and the familiar, that I rediscovered connection through postcards. I'd write to my partner about eating a wrap at university or to a friend about seeing a Highland cow. Each card became more than paper. It was a gesture of love.
