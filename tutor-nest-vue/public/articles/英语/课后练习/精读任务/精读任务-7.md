# Building optimism

In March, Smiljan Radic was awarded the 2026 Pritzker Prize, an award often called the Nobel Prize of architecture. This year's jury praised the 60-year-old Chilean architect for "his experimentation and work that embraces something fundamental about the human condition", according to Dezeen, the world's leading architecture and design magazine.

Radic's path to architecture developed gradually through experiences, doubts and discoveries. He studied architecture at Pontificia Universidad Catolica de Chile in Santiago, but failed his initial attempt at the final exam before graduating in 1989. This setback led him to study history in Venice and travel widely. These experiences introduced him to different places, cultures and building styles, shaping his understanding of architecture as a cultural and philosophical practice rather than merely a technical profession, noted ArchDaily.

As a result, his buildings are designed to fit their surroundings. Some are built partly underground, while others are positioned to block strong winds or sunlight. For example, the Pite House in Chile is designed to shield its rooms from the area's strong coastal winds and sunlight.

Radic also aims for his work to "carry emotional presence", Dezeen noted. In 2015, Radic renovated an early-twentieth-century residential heritage building that had been damaged by a natural disaster. While keeping the original structure, he added new spaces for performances, rehearsals and workshops, creating a lively atmosphere for community events. "His buildings ... provide a structured, optimistic and quietly joyful shelter," the 2026 jury citation stated.

# Fulfilled by fantasy

While traditional types of books, such as non-fiction, are becoming less popular, fantasy stories are taking over the shelves, as shown by the International Book Markets 2025 report. But what makes fantasy so hard to resist?

Fantasy is true to the human mind, especially to its wild side that we all feel but cannot easily express in our normal, controlled daily lives. Canadian philosopher Charles Taylor pointed out that everyone lives within limits that both control and comfort us in "the buffered self", which makes us see the world as lacking magic and mystery. There is a clear boundary between our inner selves and the outside world, and we don't believe in ghosts or any supernatural forces. This idea protects us from being overwhelmed by things we cannot understand or control, yet it also holds back the wildest parts of our imagination, making us hope that magic and wonder could someday return to our lives.

Besides, fantasy works as a controlled, safe return of ideas we have pushed aside, the UK author Adam Roberts noted in *Fantasy: A Short History*. It lets us experience a gentle, limited version of the legendary past. With its kings and adventures, fantasy brings back what we miss in our modern and orderly world of work and routine, but only in a controlled way. We enjoy imagining ourselves as strong heroes or special "chosen ones" when real life makes us feel small. But we do not want to live in a real world ruled by powerful kings and violent warriors.

Moreover, fantasy is something not just wanted but needed, as it encourages our imagination, noted The Guardian. A world where only physical and scientific processes exist can feel cold and empty and cannot feed our hearts and minds. This is the idea the UK author Philip Pullman expresses in his book *The Rose Field*. In the story, the character Lyra considers that human beings need certain things we cannot prove, yet we would struggle to live without them, especially our imagination. As she says: "Maybe the imagination is a sort of wind that blows through all the worlds ... It shows us true things."

# Navigating the mind

Editor's note: Laura Pigott is a neuroscientist from the UK. Her research interests include the detection of brain cancers using neuroimaging and the impact of everyday tasks, like social media, on brain development. At the age of 22, she became the youngest person to be appointed as a lecturer in the UK. Three Teens readers talked with her to learn more about neuroscience.

**Sun:** What are the research areas of neuroscience? **Pigott:** Neuroscience is the study of the brain and nervous system. Because the brain controls everything from movement to emotions and decision-making, neuroscience has multiple research areas. Some major areas include understanding how networks of brain regions work together, understanding brain diseases, and exploring how the brain develops from childhood to adulthood. Neuroscience is highly interdisciplinary, meaning it overlaps with many other fields. For example, my research area, brain imaging, might combine medicine, physics and computer science to understand how diseases change the brain.

**Sun:** What are some common experimental design approaches in neuroscience research? **Pigott:** Neuroimaging, like magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), shows brain activity in living humans. In behavioral experiments, people perform tasks that measure memory, attention and decision-making. Scientists also study neurons and genes using laboratory techniques. Animals like mice or zebrafish help us understand brain mechanisms that cannot be studied in humans, while mathematical models simulate brain processes and neural networks. Modern neuroscience usually combines several methods.

**Xia:** Is fast-updating content today related to the fragmented thinking and decreased attention span in teenagers? **Pigott:** Yes, many researchers believe that the design of fast, rapidly changing content can encourage short bursts of attention rather than sustained focus. Platforms with very rapid content turnover may encourage constant task switching, reduce practice of deep concentration and increase dopamine-driven novelty seeking. However, it's important to note that attention span is trainable. We can set specific times for social media use, read some books and long articles, and use techniques like 25-minute focused study sessions.

**Guo:** Based on your research, could you please give us some suggestions for using the brain more healthily? **Pigott:** Healthy brain habits are surprisingly simple. Sleep is essential for memory, learning and emotional regulation. Balancing online and offline life is also important. Try to include exercise, hobbies and social interactions that don't involve screens. Regular exercise is beneficial as it improves blood flow to the brain and supports neuroplasticity, the brain's ability to change and learn. Remember to avoid constant multitasking and give your brain breaks sometimes.

Hao Feifei contributed to this story.

# Seeing beyond sight

Luke, a blind Australian Shepherd, came to us seven years ago from a working horse farm. He can't see, but moves with inspiring fearlessness, compensating with astonishing smell and hearing. That's how he notices things. He seems to understand he'll occasionally run into things, but that doesn't hold him back.

Luke looks like the classic Australian Shepherd – grey, white, brown and black with spots all over. His eyes are old and filmy, but they have life in them. At home, he knows where everything is and only bumps into things when he's excited. We help by not moving things unnecessarily and keeping them tidy.

Luke loves chewing on bones and having his ears scratched. He often walks by other dogs without noticing them. Cats can easily trick him by being quiet. One cat enjoys letting Luke find him, and then moves just a bit to restart the game.

He is always happy, smiles a lot, and senses when you're down. At a stage in our lives when we're getting older and the world's bad news makes it harder to care, Luke has taught us tolerance and acceptance. Luke always cares – and reminds us that we can, too.

Last year, when I had two heart attacks, Luke was deeply upset. The time I was in the hospital, and he stuck to me after I came back. He's brought me back to life again, with his unconditional love.

When we introduce him to new people and mention that he is blind, they often shake their heads in sympathy. We once tried to explain that he lives a full and happy life, but now we simply nod and say thank you, knowing full well the genuine joy he brings to everyone around him every single day.

He is different, yet able. Each day, he shows us that it's okay to just be yourself. You don't have to be perfect; you just have to keep trying. In life, you may bump into things or even hit your head, but you can't give up. Luke never would.

— By Steven Tabarez
