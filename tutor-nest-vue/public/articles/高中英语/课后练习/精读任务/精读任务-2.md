# Boiling lobsters no more

In countries like Switzerland, Norway and New Zealand, boiling lobsters while they are alive is illegal. Now, one more country has joined the group.

On Dec 22, 2025, the UK introduced a new animal welfare strategy, which includes a ban on boiling lobsters alive. Animal welfare charities suggest more humane methods, such as stunning lobsters with an electric gun or chilling them in cold air or ice before boiling.

The new rule builds on the Animal Welfare Act passed in 2022, which recognized lobsters, crabs and other similar animals as sentient beings capable of feeling pain. This decision was supported by a government-commissioned independent review that looked at over 300 studies in 2021.

To fishermen and restaurant owners in the UK, the new policy is upsetting. They claim it will increase costs and harm the live shellfish trade. According to The Telegraph, buying stunning equipment costs around 3,500 pounds (about 33,093 yuan), which may lead restaurants to import frozen seafood. "We've got rising costs, rising wages, everything is just getting harder and harder," John Loag, the owner of a shellfish restaurant in the UK, told The Telegraph.

# Beating the sea

For many people, traveling around the world may involve planes, trains or cars. However, the Clipper Round the World Yacht Race offers a far more exciting way to travel: sailing.

The race covers about 40,000 nautical miles, crossing five oceans and facing all kinds of extreme weather and sea conditions. It has eight legs, and sailors can choose to sign up for one leg, several legs or even the whole race. All yachts sail using only wind power, making the race a true test between sailors and nature.

"Sailing without an engine lets you truly feel the rhythm of the ocean," said Lan Jun, 29, an ambassador sailor for the yacht Qingdao. She will take part in all eight legs of the 11-month journey around the world.

Lan fell in love with sailing after joining a sailing club during her college years. "To be honest, I didn't like sports when I was young," she said. "But I gradually saw the beauty in them. They have the power to shape a person."

The race is an endurance challenge like no other. One of the most difficult parts is the nonstop nature of the competition. "We race day and night to achieve the best results," Lan said. On her yacht, the crew follows a strict three-hour work and three-hour sleep schedule. "This is also for our safety, because someone must always be watching what's happening."

Sailors must also deal with extreme conditions such as strong winds, huge waves, burning heat and freezing cold. In earlier legs, one of Lan's teammates was knocked down by powerful waves and had to be taken away by helicopter. "When I saw that, I couldn't stop crying and wondered what even harder conditions might come next," she said. "**Another time I cried was when I took control of the boat and crossed the finish line** – those were tears of excitement and pride."

# Dark matter may be real

The term "dark matter", often encountered in science fiction and movies, might not be purely a product of imagination. Recently, scientists may have discovered the first real proof of dark matter: gamma rays that match the energy and pattern expected when particles in dark matter collide.

**The concept of dark matter first emerged in the 1930s when Swiss astronomer Fritz Zwicky noticed that galaxies in clusters moved too quickly to be held together by the gravity of visible matter.** Zwicky suggested that there might be an invisible form of matter that created the gravity holding these galaxies together, now known as dark matter. It is thought to make up about 27 percent of the universe's total mass energy, while ordinary matter – such as people and daily objects – makes up only about 5 percent, according to NASA.

Dark matter can't be seen because it doesn't interact with light. However, because it theoretically occupies physical space, its existence can be inferred based on its gravitational effects throughout the universe. Many scientists believe it consists of particles called weakly interacting massive particles, or WIMPs, which are thought to be many times heavier than a proton. When two WIMPs crash into each other, they might produce gamma rays that we can detect, according to NBC News.

Using NASA's Fermi gamma-ray space telescope, a research team looked into the regions of the Milky Way and found gamma rays with energy as high as 20 billion electronvolts. **These rays match what scientists expect if WIMPs were to collide.** The rays also form a halo-like shape near the center of the Milky Way galaxy, similar to what a dark matter halo might look like. This pattern doesn't fit well with other known sources or typical space events. If this finding is correct, it could be the first time humans have actually "seen" dark matter.

Meanwhile, another recent study has also found clues to prove the existence of dark matter. Scientists used supercomputers to map dark matter in the Milky Way galaxy. The resulting simulated maps matched gamma ray data from the Fermi telescope, suggesting that excess gamma rays in the galaxy's center could be from dark matter.

The findings of the two studies are meaningful. If scientists can confirm dark matter's existence, "it would be a total game changer, because it really is something that seems to dominate the universe," professor David Kaplan at Johns Hopkins University told NBC News.

# Breaking down walls

Kollmar is not only a student but also the seventh generation of a family-owned German packaging paper machinery company founded in 1842. During his six-day visit to China, he did more than just go sightseeing.
